## 1. Research Question

**What do the latest global aviation safety results tell us about the current safety environment, and what should aviation stakeholders pay attention to beyond the headline accident statistics?**

### Intelligence Objective

To demonstrate how HAF transforms current aviation safety information from authoritative sources into structured, contextualized and decision-useful aviation intelligence.

---

# 2. Step 1 — IDENTIFY

### Intelligence Need

Aviation safety performance is often communicated through headline indicators such as accident rates, fatal accidents and fatalities.

However, individual indicators can produce different conclusions.

For example:

- The number of accidents may decrease.
- The accident rate may improve.
- Fatal accidents may decrease.
- Yet total fatalities may increase.

The intelligence problem is therefore not simply:

> **“Is aviation becoming safer?”**

It is:

> **“What does the underlying safety data actually tell us, and where should safety attention remain focused?”**

### Key Questions

1. What changed in global aviation safety performance during 2025?
2. Which safety indicators improved?
3. Which indicators deteriorated?
4. Which accident categories remain the highest-risk?
5. What systemic weaknesses are identified by ICAO?
6. What does this mean for safety management and safety intelligence?
7. What should aviation stakeholders monitor going forward?

---

# 3. Step 2 — COLLECT

The research prioritizes authoritative aviation sources.

### Primary Sources

**ICAO**

- ICAO 2026 Safety Report / 2025 safety data
- ICAO Safety Management information
- ICAO Annex 19 Safety Management developments

**IATA**

- 2025 Annual Safety Report
- IATA accident and safety performance data

**EASA**

- European Plan for Aviation Safety (EPAS) 2026
- Current safety information and emerging-risk publications

### Source Selection Principle

HAF gives priority to:

1. International aviation organizations
2. National aviation authorities
3. Official accident investigation authorities
4. Aircraft manufacturers / operators where relevant
5. Peer-reviewed research
6. Reputable industry sources
7. General media as secondary or lead sources only

---

# 4. Step 3 — VERIFY

## Confirmed Global Safety Indicators

According to ICAO’s 2026 Safety Report covering 2025:

- Approximately **5 billion passengers** travelled on commercial air services in 2025.
- Approximately **38 million flights** were operated.
- Total accidents decreased from **95 in 2024 to 85 in 2025**.
- The global accident rate improved from **2.6 to 2.2 accidents per million departures**.
- Fatal accidents decreased from **10 to 4**.
- The fatal accident rate declined from **0.27 to 0.11 per million departures**.
- Despite this improvement, total fatalities increased from **296 to 387**.
- The fatality rate increased from **65 to 78 fatalities per billion passengers**.

These figures demonstrate why single safety indicators should not be interpreted in isolation.

### Verification Note

The ICAO data concern scheduled commercial air transport involving aeroplanes with a maximum take-off mass above 5,700 kg.

Therefore, the results should **not automatically be generalized to all aviation sectors**.

---

# 5. Step 4 — CONTEXTUALIZE

The apparent contradiction between improved accident rates and increased fatalities is explained by the statistical impact of rare catastrophic events.

ICAO reports that one accident in 2025 accounted for **260 fatalities**, substantially influencing the annual fatality total.

This is important from an aviation safety intelligence perspective.

### Safety Data ≠ Safety Intelligence

A raw statement such as:

> “The accident rate improved.”

is a **fact**.

But it is not yet sufficient intelligence.

The intelligence emerges when the result is placed in context:

> Accident frequency improved significantly, but the occurrence of a small number of catastrophic events can still produce substantial changes in annual fatality outcomes.

This distinction is central to HAF.

---

# 6. Step 5 — ANALYZE

## 6.1 Frequency Risk vs Consequence Risk

The 2025 data demonstrate two different dimensions of aviation safety risk:

### Frequency

How often does an undesirable event occur?

### Consequence

How severe can the outcome become when the event occurs?

A system can improve considerably in preventing accidents while still remaining exposed to very high-consequence events.

Therefore:

> **Reducing event frequency does not eliminate catastrophic consequence risk.**

---

## 6.2 High-Risk Categories

ICAO identifies five Global High-Risk Categories of Occurrences:

- Controlled Flight Into Terrain (CFIT)
- Loss of Control in Flight (LOC-I)
- Mid-Air Collision (MAC)
- Runway Excursion (RE)
- Runway Incursion (RI)

Together, these categories accounted for more than half of all fatal accidents in 2025.

LOC-I received particular attention because it represented nearly one-third of fatal accidents within ICAO’s high-risk categories.

CFIT also remained significant because a single CFIT accident accounted for more than 10% of worldwide fatalities.

MAC occurrences represented another important area of concern, accounting for nearly 15% of fatalities worldwide in 2025.

---

## 6.3 Positive Signal: Runway Safety

ICAO reported **no runway-incursion accidents involving commercial air transport in 2025**.

However, seven runway-excursion accidents occurred, including one involving fatal injuries.

### Intelligence Interpretation

This suggests that runway safety should not be treated as one homogeneous risk.

Runway incursion and runway excursion require different risk controls, operational defenses and monitoring indicators.

A single “runway safety” KPI can therefore conceal important differences between risk categories.

---

# 7. Step 6 — SYNTHESIZE

## HAF Intelligence Assessment

### Finding 1 — Global aviation safety performance improved in frequency terms.

The decline in overall accidents and fatal accident rates represents measurable improvement.

### Finding 2 — Catastrophic risk remains disproportionately important.

The increase in total fatalities despite fewer fatal accidents demonstrates that rare events can dominate annual consequences.

### Finding 3 — Safety management must look beyond aggregate indicators.

Organizations that monitor only total accident frequency may miss important changes in high-consequence risk.

### Finding 4 — High-risk occurrence categories remain appropriate strategic priorities.

CFIT, LOC-I, MAC, RE and RI continue to provide useful structures for targeted safety risk management.

### Finding 5 — Safety oversight remains a critical system-level factor.

ICAO reported a worldwide average safety-oversight score of **69%** in 2025.

ICAO identified particular weaknesses involving:

- resolution of identified safety issues;
- availability of qualified technical personnel; and
- effective continuing safety surveillance and inspections.

---

# 8. Step 7 — COMMUNICATE

## Executive Intelligence Brief

### Global Aviation Safety: The Numbers Improved — But the Risk Picture Is More Complex

Global commercial aviation recorded fewer accidents and substantially fewer fatal accidents in 2025.

The global accident rate improved from 2.6 to 2.2 accidents per million departures, while the fatal accident rate declined from 0.27 to 0.11.

However, total fatalities increased from 296 to 387.

The key lesson is therefore not that aviation safety deteriorated.

Nor is it sufficient to conclude simply that safety improved.

The more useful conclusion is:

> **Aviation is becoming increasingly effective at preventing accidents, but the residual risk associated with rare, catastrophic events remains a critical safety-management challenge.**

For aviation organizations, this reinforces the need to combine:

- occurrence data;
- risk severity;
- high-risk categories;
- safety intelligence;
- operational context;
- human-performance factors; and
- effectiveness of mitigations.

The objective should be not merely to count events, but to understand **where the remaining risk resides and whether existing defenses are working.**

---

# 9. Step 8 — REVIEW

## Limitations

This analysis should not be interpreted as a prediction of future accident rates.

Annual aviation safety statistics are sensitive to individual events, particularly when accident frequency is very low.

In addition:

- some accident investigations may remain incomplete;
- classifications can change as additional evidence becomes available;
- different aviation datasets use different definitions and populations;
- ICAO and IATA datasets should not be treated as automatically interchangeable.

IATA notes that accident classifications and contributing factors can change as investigations develop and that historical data may be updated.

Therefore, HAF treats safety intelligence as a **living analytical product**, subject to review when significant new evidence becomes available.

---

# 10. Decision-Relevant Implications

Based on the evidence reviewed, aviation stakeholders should consider monitoring:

### For Operators

- High-risk occurrence categories
- Effectiveness of existing mitigations
- Human-performance vulnerabilities
- Operational trend indicators
- Safety-reporting quality
- Leading and lagging safety indicators

### For Regulators

- Resolution of identified safety issues
- Availability of qualified technical personnel
- Continuing surveillance effectiveness
- State Safety Programme performance
- Safety-risk trends across high-risk categories

### For Safety Departments

Safety dashboards should avoid relying exclusively on accident counts.

A stronger approach is to combine:

**Frequency + Severity + Exposure + Trend + Risk Category + Mitigation Effectiveness**

---

# 11. HAF Intelligence Conclusion

The most important insight from the 2025 global safety data is not a single accident-rate number.

It is the relationship between:

**frequency → consequence → exposure → risk → mitigation → residual risk**

This is where aviation safety data becomes aviation safety intelligence.

The data indicate significant improvement in accident and fatal-accident rates, while also demonstrating that rare catastrophic events can continue to dominate safety outcomes.

Consequently, the next stage of aviation safety improvement will require increasingly sophisticated use of safety data and safety intelligence to identify where residual risk remains and where interventions can produce the greatest safety benefit.
